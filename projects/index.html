Project Theme

The Value of Expertise in the Age of AI

In a world where information is increasingly accessible, expertise is a particularly complex factor to consider in how we design. Everyone has expertise (or more broadly, specialized skill) in something, whether they’re preeminent researchers defining a field or everyday parents who have learned a thing or two after years of raising kids.

There are many kinds of expertise, and many ways to develop it:

  • Some people develop expertise through rigorous practice or study, like a professor building a career on their specific research, a firefighter or EMT who goes through extensive training, or a professional athlete who constantly works to hone their skills.
  • Some people hone a skill for their own personal enjoyment and develop expertise incidentally, such as hobby artists and crafters, recreational fishers, or amateur skateboarders.
  • Other people become experts unintentionally, through lived experience, such as a “local” who knows the ins and outs of the city they’ve always lived in, or a blind person with years of “lived expertise” in nonvisual navigation.

When developing expertise in a specific area, it is often important to specialize one’s process, rather than defaulting to a more general approach. For instance, a boxer, a swimmer, and a football player will each have their own specialized workout regimen, all of which differ from general “fitness” routines. In order to succeed, designs in this space must be tailored to their specific audience and domain, thoughtfully navigating the differences between the specialized practice and more generalized alternatives.

There are also many goals related to expertise: some people lack expertise and aim to acquire it; others are experts and wish to effectively apply it; and still others aim to share their expertise and teach non-experts.

Recent advancements in generative AI have surfaced a specific tension related to expertise: while many argue GenAI makes it easier to become proficient at a skill, others might argue that “shortcutting” the skill-building process with AI fails to build the same expertise. In considering how to design for expertise in 2026, how do you reconcile the potential benefits of AI while still accounting for its limitations?

  • Project instructions for teams

  • Project Brief:

    • Pick a specific area of expertise or specialization. This can be a skill, a study, a profession, a craft, an activity... anything that a person can develop expertise in, but try to avoid broad/general areas that people commonly focus on or that are overrepresented in design.
    • Consider the people who might have this expertise or want to develop it themselves.
    • Identify design challenges related to gaining, sharing, applying, combining, refining, or otherwise interacting with this specific area of expertise.

    Throughout your project, consider where Generative AI and other intelligent systems could be employed, and be intentional in your decisions to include OR exclude these systems in your design.
